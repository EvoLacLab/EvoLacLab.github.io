# Evolutionary Lactation Lab

The EvoLac Lab is a research group dedicated to advancing the understanding of lactation biology and its impact on human health through an evolutionary biology framework. Our work focuses on the impact of environmental stressors on breastfeeding and milk composition with the aim to improve maternal and child welfare around the world. We are an interdisciplinary and international group, fostering a supportive and inclusive environment that values curiosity, collaboration, and innovation.

## Highlights

{% capture text %}

Milk is often treated as just another food item, but this mindset overlooks the true nature of lactation, which is the product of millions of years of evolution, shaped as a compromise between maternal and offspring health. Many mothers who plan to breastfeed feel they are not making enough milk, yet this difficulty is still not treated as a medical condition: there are no standard tests, no agreed figures on how common it is, and no clear route to help. All too often the problem is blamed on breastfeeding technique, even though research points to other causes: metabolic conditions, poverty, social exclusion, and ongoing stress. 

Yet, maternal milk is the first nutrition every mammal consumes, and evidence shows breastfeeding can leave a lasting footprint on development and lifelong health, for mother and infant alike. So why do we still understand so little about what drives its composition? 

To narrow this gap, our group studies the ecology of maternal milk as a biological system fine-tuned to the environment that mother and offspring face. At the centre of our work is the preliminary evidence that milk composition may differ depending on the sex of the baby especially in the context of disadvantage. We are therefore asking how environmental pressures interact with the sex of the infant to shape lactation physiology. To uncover milk's "compositional fingerprints", we use advanced metabolomic and proteomic techniques alongside sophisticated statistical modelling, searching for hormonal and metabolic signatures that may influence infant growth and lifelong health.  

At the same time, driven by the goal to improve the breastfeeding support for mothers and babies worldwide, we are testing whether simple biomarkers of lactation problems, such as sodium levels in milk, could be applied clinically to direct the support mothers receive. 

Finally, we compare our human findings with our unique comparative model: the Asian elephant, an endangered species that shares many life-history traits with humans and suffers high rates of calf mortality. Beyond serving as a scientific model, our team has developed a deep appreciation for these animals and actively contributes to research relevant to their welfare and conservation.

MilkINESS — Milk, Parental Investment and Sexual Selection 

Why do some mothers struggle to produce enough milk while others do not? MilkINESS investigates how different forms of socio-environmental stress and deprivation shape breastfeeding behaviour, lactation physiology and milk composition, with particular attention to the sex of the offspring. The project also examines whether diagnostic tools for lactation problems are feasible and useful in clinical practice. Two human cohorts are currently being recruited: mothers seeking sanctuary in Wales, together with a comparison group, and mothers experiencing high work-to-family conflict in Japan, again with a comparison group. We are continually exploring new settings in which suboptimal living conditions affect mothers,  and would love to hear from anyone that has new ideas and/or access to additional deprivation settings. 

EMERALD — Evaluating Milk Replacements for Hand-Reared Asian Elephants 

Orphaned Asian elephant calves raised by hand frequently die from malnutrition, yet the milk replacers used to feed them have never been systematically compared with the milk their mothers produce. EMERALD will deliver the first metabolomic profiling of both maternal Asian elephant milk and the commercially available replacers used in captivity. Mapping the two against each other will show precisely where replacers fall short of maternal milk, informing evidence-based nutrition protocols and improving welfare for this endangered species.

The EvoLac Lab brings together scientists from diverse disciplines united by a single question: how does environment shape what mothers make for their babies? Our members span fields that rarely meet in one room- lactation physiology, metabolomics, environmental health, public health, and veterinary science - and we believe the most important discoveries happen at these intersections. 

We work across continents, recruiting mothers from Wales to Japan and collaborating with sanctuaries caring for endangered Asian elephants. This geographic reach reflects something deeper: we view lactation as both a biological process and a lived experience, shaped by culture, circumstances. 

Beyond the data, we're people who care deeply about the subjects we study. The mothers who share their stories with us, the babies whose health depends on better understanding of milk, the orphaned calves - none of them are abstract categories. 

Our growing team welcomes visiting researchers, postdoctoral fellows, and PhD students who want to do science that connects molecular detail with real-world impact. Whether you come from wet-lab biochemistry, computational modelling, or social science, there's space here to contribute to a research programme that treats lactation as the complex, evolved, deeply mammalian system it truly is.
